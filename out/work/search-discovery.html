The products weren't inconsistent. The experience was.

01 · The Situation

The Situation

The page worked. But the experience didn't always feel connected.

A Product Listing Page brings together multiple systems: product results, filters, sorting, sponsored content, seller information, recommendations, and merchandising.

Individually, these elements may function as intended. But across the page, similar information and interactions can be presented differently.

The challenge was not simply to introduce another feature or redesign the entire page.

It was to identify where the experience lacked consistency, clarity, or predictability.

“The opportunity wasn't to redesign everything. It was to understand where the experience stopped behaving like one system.”
02 · Finding the Friction

Finding the Friction

Looking beyond individual components.

I examined the PLP as a connected experience, looking for places where customers might encounter similar information or actions presented in different ways.

Areas of investigation included:

  • Product information hierarchy
  • Filtering and sorting
  • Third-party seller visibility
  • Sponsored and promoted content
  • Carousels and recommendations
  • Interaction patterns across the experience

The goal was to identify inconsistencies that could affect comprehension, discoverability, or decision-making.

Design Question

Where are customers being asked to relearn patterns that should already feel familiar?

03 · Defining the Opportunity

Defining the Opportunity

Not every difference is a problem.

Consistency does not mean making every component identical.

A difference becomes worth investigating when it introduces unnecessary effort, uncertainty, or an unexpected interaction.

I framed the audit around three questions:

  • <strong>UNDERSTANDABLE</strong> — Can customers quickly recognize what they are looking at and what actions are available?
  • <strong>PREDICTABLE</strong> — Do similar interactions behave consistently across the experience?
  • <strong>INTENTIONAL</strong> — When patterns differ, is there a meaningful reason grounded in the customer's context?
“Consistency matters where expectations are shared. Context matters where needs differ.”
04 · Looking Beyond Our PLP

Looking Beyond Our PLP

Researching patterns, not copying competitors.

I reviewed comparable e-commerce experiences to understand how other retailers approached similar challenges.

The exploration focused on product presentation, filtering, seller information, promoted content, and discovery interactions.

I used these references to distinguish between:

  • <strong>Established conventions</strong> — Patterns customers may already recognize.
  • <strong>Emerging approaches</strong> — Ideas worth exploring further.
  • <strong>Context-dependent solutions</strong> — Patterns that may work elsewhere but need evaluation before adoption.
“Competitive research gave us reference points, not ready-made answers.”
05 · From Observations to Opportunities

From Observations to Opportunities

Turning inconsistencies into design directions.

Organizing the design opportunities into three concise themes:

These are design opportunity areas, not claims of proven customer problems or completed outcomes.

  • <strong>01 — CLARIFY INFORMATION HIERARCHY</strong>: Explore how important product and seller information can be easier to recognize and compare.
  • <strong>02 — STRENGTHEN INTERACTION CONSISTENCY</strong>: Identify opportunities for similar customer actions to follow more predictable patterns.
  • <strong>03 — IMPROVE DISCOVERY WITHOUT ADDING NOISE</strong>: Explore how promoted, recommended, and third-party content can be surfaced without competing excessively with the primary shopping experience.
06 · Exploring Third-Party Discovery

Exploring Third-Party Discovery

Making additional choices easier to explore.

One area explored within the broader PLP work was the presentation and discovery of third-party products.

Design Question

How might we improve the visibility and exploration of third-party products without disrupting the primary shopping journey?

This required balancing discoverability, information hierarchy, customer control, and the surrounding PLP experience.

07 · Design Explorations

Design Explorations

Different directions. Different trade-offs.

Presenting design alternatives as explorations of specific decisions rather than simply a collection of screens:

  • <strong>EXPLORATION A — VISIBILITY</strong>: Explore how the component could become easier to notice while remaining integrated into the PLP.
  • <strong>EXPLORATION B — NAVIGATION</strong>: Explore how customers could browse relevant products without unnecessary interruption.
  • <strong>EXPLORATION C — CONTROL</strong>: Explore lightweight ways to help customers navigate available options, including concepts such as Top-rated sellers · Most relevant · Most discounted.

These are explored design concepts. They do not imply that all directions were implemented or validated.

08 · Designing for the Whole Page

Designing for the Whole Page

A better component still has to work inside a busy PLP.

A carousel or filtering pattern cannot be evaluated only in isolation.

Its placement and behavior also affect the surrounding product results, information hierarchy, promoted content, and scrolling experience.

The design direction focused on giving relevant content enough presence to be discoverable without allowing it to dominate the customer's primary task.

“The goal wasn't simply to make a component more visible. It was to make it fit the experience.”
09 · Design Outcomes & Direction

Design Outcomes & Direction

From isolated observations to more intentional patterns.

The work established design directions focused on:

  • <strong>CLEARER HIERARCHY</strong>: Making important information easier to scan and interpret.
  • <strong>MORE PREDICTABLE INTERACTIONS</strong>: Encouraging consistency across similar customer actions.
  • <strong>BETTER DISCOVERY</strong>: Exploring ways to surface third-party content without overwhelming the primary experience.
  • <strong>REUSABLE DESIGN THINKING</strong>: Considering how individual decisions could inform patterns across the PLP.

Framing Note: These represent design directions and intended benefits rather than measured business outcomes or completed production deployments.

10 · Reflection

Reflection

Consistency isn't about making everything identical.

This project reinforced the importance of examining the relationships between components rather than treating each screen or interaction as an isolated design problem.

A more consistent experience comes from understanding where customers expect familiar behavior—and where context genuinely calls for something different.

The strongest design decisions are not always about adding something new. Sometimes they're about making what already exists work better together.
