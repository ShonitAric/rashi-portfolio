Making Fieldwork Easier before the first knock on the door

01 · Overview

Overview

One appointment. A lot happening behind it.

Worksite supports field teams as they move through delivery, repair, installation, and other appointment-related workflows.

The challenge wasn't simply redesigning an Appointment Detail Page. It was understanding what information an agent needs, when they need it, and what action should come next as an appointment moves through different stages.

My work focuses on restructuring that experience—from understanding the end-to-end journey and information architecture to exploring interaction patterns and evolving the experience through low- and higher-fidelity designs.

02 · The Challenge

The Challenge

The information was there. Finding what mattered was harder.

The Appointment Details Page (ADP) is one of the most frequently used screens inside Work Site.

It acts as the operational hub where technicians access customer information, products, services, notes, documentation, and workflow actions.

As part of a modernization initiative, leadership wanted to improve technician efficiency and reduce workflow friction across the appointment lifecycle.

At first, the ask sounded straightforward:

“Redesign the Appointment Details Page.”

But after reviewing workflows and speaking with stakeholders, I realized the problem wasn't the screen itself.

The problem was much larger.

Appointment Details was being used across multiple stages of a technician's journey, yet the information hierarchy remained largely static regardless of context.

The Real Question

“How might Appointment Details adapt to where a technician is in their workflow?”

Because what a technician needs while:

  • Preparing for a pre-call
  • Leaving the warehouse
  • Traveling to an appointment
  • Performing a repair
  • Completing documentation

...is completely different.

The challenge wasn't redesigning a page.

The challenge was designing a system that could support changing needs throughout the technician journey.

04 · What I Learned

What I Learned

Three patterns emerged almost immediately.

01 Context Changes Constantly

Information useful during a pre-call is not the same information needed in a warehouse.

Likewise, information needed during a repair differs from information required during appointment completion.

02 Not Everything Deserves Equal Attention

The existing experience surfaced large amounts of information at once.

Important details often competed with secondary information.

03 The Next Action Matters More Than The Data

Technicians don't open ADP to read information. They open it to decide:

“What should I do next?”
05 · Key Insight

Key Insight

The information wasn't missing. Finding what mattered was hard.

The existing experience already contained most of the information technicians needed.

The challenge was helping them:

  • Find it faster
  • Understand it quicker
  • Act on it confidently

This shifted the project from feature design to information design.

06 · Creating A Stage-Based Information Model

Creating A Stage-Based Information Model

Technicians don't need all data at all times. In the field, cognitive overload directly slows service velocity.

Instead of treating Appointment Details as a static record, I restructured it as a stage-aware operational hub categorized into distinct information layers:

01 · Persistent Layer (Anchor Data — Always Present)

Core data technicians constantly reference regardless of journey phase. Anchored at the top for immediate recognition:

  • <strong>Customer Identity & Quick Contact:</strong> Name, verified phone, address, access codes.
  • <strong>Appointment Time Window:</strong> Current scheduled arrival slot and service status.
  • <strong>Primary Job Scope:</strong> Core work order type (Delivery, Repair, Install).

02 · Contextual Layer (Stage-Aware — Dynamically Surfaced)

Surfaces only the actions and instructions required for the technician's immediate physical stage:

  • <strong>Pre-Call Stage:</strong> Customer availability confirmation, special access notes.
  • <strong>Warehouse Departure:</strong> Part verification, staging checklist, loading confirmation.
  • <strong>In-Transit:</strong> Turn-by-turn navigation shortcut, gate codes, running-late quick notification.
  • <strong>On-Site Execution:</strong> Diagnosis checklist, warranty check, part replacement logging.
  • <strong>Job Wrap-Up:</strong> Digital signature capture, payment collection, return staging.

Appointment-Specific Information

Changes depending on:

  • Delivery
  • Repair
  • Installation
  • Pickup
  • Haul Away

Supporting Information

Available when needed but shouldn't dominate primary tasks. Examples:

  • Notes
  • Documentation
  • Photos
  • History
07 · Design Principles

Design Principles

The resulting framework was guided by four principles.

Persistent

Keep essential information accessible throughout the journey.

Contextual

Adapt information based on technician needs.

Expandable

Make supporting information available without overwhelming the primary experience.

Actionable

Prioritize information that helps technicians decide what to do next.

08 · Applying The Framework

Applying The Framework

Pre-call became the first stage used to validate the model.

Instead of designing for every possible scenario at once, I focused on understanding how information hierarchy could better support technician goals during appointment preparation.

This exploration became the foundation for upcoming stages including:

  • Warehouse
  • Transit
  • Start Appointment
  • In Appointment
  • Completion

Each stage uses the same underlying framework while adapting content priority and actions to match technician needs.

09 · Example: Pre-call

Example: Pre-call

During a pre-call, technicians are primarily trying to answer four questions:

  • Who am I calling?
  • Why am I calling?
  • What is the appointment about?
  • What action should I take?

The existing experience contained the required information but required excessive scanning.

I explored:

  • Separating customer context from appointment context
  • Reorganizing information around technician goals
  • Reducing competing actions
  • Improving content hierarchy
  • Supporting multiple note-capture methods

The goal was not to simplify the workflow.

The goal was to simplify understanding.

10 · Outcome

Outcome

The project established a scalable framework for how Appointment Details can evolve across the technician journey rather than being redesigned as a single isolated screen.

So far, the work has delivered:

  • Stage-based JTBDs
  • Context-aware information hierarchy
  • A scalable ADP framework
  • Improved content prioritization
  • Reduced cognitive load
  • A foundation for future appointment stages

Most importantly, it changed the conversation from:

“What features should we add?”

to

“What information matters right now?”
11 · Reflection

Reflection

One of the biggest lessons from this project was recognizing that enterprise experiences often suffer from information overload rather than missing functionality.

By stepping back from the screen and understanding the technician journey first, I was able to create a framework that scales across multiple workflows while preserving the behaviors technicians already know and trust.

In the end, this project wasn't about redesigning a page.

It was about designing for decision-making.
